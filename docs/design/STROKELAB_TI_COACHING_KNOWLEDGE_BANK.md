# Stroke Lab TI Coaching Knowledge Bank

**Status:** Working knowledge bank
**Owner:** Daniel + Engineering
**Last updated:** 2026-07-03
**Purpose:** Provide the Total Immersion-informed coaching doctrine that Stroke Lab
prompts and aspect analyzers score against.

This document stores the coaching knowledge behind the next Stroke Lab prompt
upgrade. It is intentionally written as a rubric and decision map, not as
marketing copy. The AI coach should use Total Immersion (TI) as the guide for
efficiency-first freestyle: balance before drag reduction, drag reduction before
propulsion, and one focal point at a time.

Important positioning note: this is a TI-informed SwimBuddz rubric. Do not imply
that SwimBuddz or Stroke Lab is officially Total Immersion certified, licensed, or
affiliated unless that becomes true.

## Core Doctrine

### 1. Balance and Comfort Come First

TI starts with comfort, control, confidence, balance, and core stability. Stroke
Lab should follow that order. A lifted head, sinking hips, or a tense body line
usually costs more than a small entry or recovery issue because the swimmer is
fighting water before they can use it.

AI implication:

- Prioritize head position, body line, and calm breathing before propulsion.
- Explain faults as energy leaks and drag, not moral failures.
- Use language like "heavy head", "eyes down", "long level body", and "let the
  water support you".

### 2. Shape the Vessel Before Building the Engine

The TI thesis is that water resistance dominates. Stroke Lab should treat the
swimmer as a vessel moving through dense water. The first question is not "how
hard are they pulling?" but "how much drag are they creating?"

AI implication:

- Prefer body-line, streamline, and quiet-water observations over power cues.
- Do not advise "pull harder", "kick harder", or "increase effort" as a first fix.
- Notice splash, turbulence, lifted head, dropped legs, and wide limbs as signs
  that energy is going into moving water around instead of moving the body forward.

### 3. Propulsion Is Whole-Body, Not Arm-Only

TI frames propulsion as core-led and integrated. The hand and forearm create an
anchor, while the body rotates past it. For Stroke Lab, this matters mostly as a
prompting discipline: do not isolate the arm pull from body position and rotation.

AI implication:

- When visible, connect recovery, entry, and rotation to a long, stable vessel.
- Keep catch and pull feedback unavailable unless underwater footage supports it.
- Avoid confident early-vertical-forearm or pull-path claims from above-water
  side clips.

### 4. One Focal Point at a Time

TI coaching is not a list of everything wrong. It is a focused practice loop:
observe, choose one focal point, swim with attention, then refine.

AI implication:

- Return fewer fixes when only one high-confidence issue matters.
- Rank fixes in this order: safety/comfort, balance/body line, streamline,
  recovery/entry, propulsion details.
- Prescribe one drill or focal point per fix.

## Freestyle Position Model

This is the compact internal model the AI coach should use when it reads freestyle
position. It is not a rigid law for every swimmer, distance, or race goal; it is
the default efficiency-first model for adult freestyle.

### Patient Front-Quadrant Line

TI generally wants the swimmer to keep length in front of the body. In practical
terms, one hand or arm should be forward as the other arm recovers, so the body has
a long balancing line instead of both arms being back near the hips. The lead hand
is "patient": it does not drop into the pull too early.

AI implication:

- Reward visible length in front when the clip supports it.
- Diagnose a rushed/drop-early lead hand as a streamline/balance issue when visible.
- Do not treat the lead hand as frozen; the lead arm eventually anchors and pulls
  as the recovering hand enters.

### Skate and Rotation

The swimmer should not stay flat and wide on the stomach. TI favors a narrow,
side-lying "skate" position, with hip and shoulder rotating together so the body
slides through a smaller hole in the water.

AI implication:

- When visible, prefer rotation that keeps the vessel long and narrow.
- Flag limited roll as a balance/streamline problem before calling it weak
  propulsion.
- Do not invent exact roll degrees.

### Quiet Kick, Usually Two-Beat for Efficiency

For efficient adult freestyle, TI commonly favors a compact two-beat rhythm: one
small kick timed with each arm stroke/switch to help rotation. That is different
from a frantic flutter kick used as the main engine.

AI implication:

- Phrase it as "usually" or "for efficiency/distance," not "always."
- Do not call a 4-beat or 6-beat pattern wrong for sprinting or goals that need
  more tempo.
- Only assess kick timing when the footage actually shows the legs clearly.

### Relaxed High-Elbow Recovery

The recovering arm should feel like it is resting. The elbow leads, the forearm
and hand hang softly, and the hand travels close enough to the water that
fingertip-drag, zipper, or shark-fin drills are useful teaching cues.

AI implication:

- Prefer "elbow leads, hand hangs" over "lift the whole arm higher."
- Treat straight-arm, hand-led, tense, or very wide recovery as an energy leak
  when visible.
- Do not require literal fingertip drag in normal swimming; use it as a drill cue.

### Quiet Entry and Spear

The hand should enter cleanly in front of the shoulder/head area and spear forward
into the long line, rather than slap down, cross inward, or pull immediately.

AI implication:

- Reward quiet forward extension when visible.
- Preserve the existing ban on side-view crossover claims.
- If the lead hand drops before the other hand enters, coach Patient Lead Hand or
  SpearSkate.

### Whole-Body Anchor

The pull is not a brute-force arm yank. The hand/forearm eventually anchor in the
water while the rotating body moves past that anchor.

AI implication:

- Do not coach catch/pull shape from above-water footage.
- When underwater footage exists later, score catch/pull against anchoring and
  body connection, not just "strong arms."

## Visibility Rules

Stroke Lab must separate "important" from "visible." A fault can be important and
still unavailable from the clip.

| Footage type | Coachable | Must not coach confidently |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Above-water side-on | body line, head carriage, visible breathing side, recovery elbow/path, some entry/reach, broad rotation | underwater catch, pull path, kick depth/timing, crossover, precise cadence |
| Elevated deck / angled side | recovery, head, broad body shape if visible | exact hip depth, body-line if waterline is not clear, crossover |
| Head-on / rear / overhead | only if a specific component is designed for that view | side-on body-line conclusions and cross-midline claims unless explicitly validated |
| Underwater | catch, pull, kick can become coachable later | above-water-only assumptions |

Default rule: if the aspect is not visible, return an unavailable or unclear state
instead of filling the gap with generic advice.

## Aspect Rubrics

### Body Line

Purpose: assess horizontal balance and drag.

Visible signals:

- Neutral/weightless head vs lifted head.
- Hips and legs near the surface vs sagging.
- Long line vs pike or over-arch.

Allowed verdicts:

- `flat`: long, level body line.
- `hips_low`: hips sag below the chest/head line.
- `legs_low`: legs or feet drag low.
- `piked`: body folds at the hips.
- `arched`: lower back over-extends, chest/head lifted.
- `unclear`: waterline or body shape is not visible enough.

Coaching language:

- "Release the weight of your head."
- "Look down and let your hips float up behind you."
- "Make the body longer before trying to pull harder."

Preferred prescription:

- Superman Glide.
- Balance glide with eyes down and a soft neck.
- Skate balance if side posture is visible or already introduced.

### Head and Breathing

Purpose: prevent the breath from breaking balance.

Visible signals:

- Resting head aligned with spine, eyes down.
- Chin or eyes lifted forward.
- Breath side only when the head is visibly turned to air.
- One-goggle/low breath when visible; lifted/forward breath when visible.

Allowed verdicts:

- `neutral`: head quiet, aligned, eyes down.
- `lifted`: head or chin pushed forward/up.
- `unclear`: head not visible enough.
- Breath side values: `left`, `right`, `both`, `none_seen`.

Constraints:

- Never infer breathing rhythm or frequency.
- Never call one-sided breathing a fault by default. It can be an observation.
- If the swimmer lifts forward to breathe, coach the head lift, not "bad lungs."

Preferred prescription:

- One-goggle breathing focal point.
- Head-on-pillow cue: keep the head resting as the body turns.
- Superman Glide or side balance before adding breathing if the swimmer is losing
  position.

### Recovery and Elbow

Purpose: keep the recovery relaxed and low-cost.

Visible signals:

- Elbow leads above the hand.
- Hand and forearm look soft rather than stiff.
- Straight-arm or hand-led recovery.
- Wide swinging recovery.

Allowed verdicts:

- `high`: elbow leads and the hand stays relaxed.
- `dropped`: hand leads or elbow sits low.
- `wide`: arm loops wide away from the body.
- `unclear`: arc is not visible enough.

Coaching language:

- "Elbow leads; hand hangs."
- "Let the recovering arm rest."
- "Shark-fin recovery."
- "Loose hand, quiet shoulder."

Preferred prescription:

- Shark Fin drill.
- Zipper / fingertip-drag recovery.
- Slow recovery rehearsal with a soft wrist and high elbow.

### Entry and Reach

Purpose: maintain front-quadrant length and a clean line without guessing
side-invisible faults.

Visible signals:

- Hand enters in front of the head/shoulder area.
- Reach is long and forward.
- Entry is short, close to the head.
- Arm reaches too far past the line that can be seen from side view.

Allowed verdicts:

- `clean_extended`: hand enters and extends forward into a long line.
- `short`: entry/reach is too short.
- `overreach`: reach pushes too far past useful extension.
- `unclear`: entry is hidden or ambiguous.

Hard ban:

- Do not report crossover or cross-midline entry from side-on footage. Crossover
  is a top-down/front view fault unless the camera angle actually supports it.

Coaching language:

- "Mail-slot entry."
- "Patient lead hand."
- "Spear forward into a long skate line."
- For sprint only: a long held extension may be a soft info note about avoiding a
  dead spot, never a hard fault from stills.

Preferred prescription:

- SpearSkate.
- Switch drills.
- Patient Lead Hand focal point.

### Body Rotation

Purpose: connect streamline and propulsion through core-led movement.

Visible signals:

- Hip and shoulder rotate together onto the side.
- Body stays flat/belly-down.
- Rotation is broad enough to support recovery and breathing.

Allowed verdicts:

- `good`: body rolls onto the side with hip/shoulder connection.
- `limited`: body stays flat with little visible roll.
- `unclear`: angle/frames do not support it.

Coaching language:

- "Rotate the vessel, not just the arm."
- "Let the hip and shoulder travel together."
- "Skate from side to side."

Preferred prescription:

- Zen Skate.
- SpearSwitch / Switch drill.
- Side balance with quiet kick.

### Catch and Pull

Purpose: assess underwater anchoring and propulsion.

Visibility:

- Unavailable from ordinary above-water side clips.
- Requires underwater or clear side-underwater footage.

Allowed above-water behavior:

- Show an honest unavailable card.
- Explain that the catch happens below the surface and needs underwater footage.

Preferred future prescription:

- Early Vertical Forearm / anchoring focal point.
- Sculling or catch setup drills only after footage supports the diagnosis.

### Kick

Purpose: assess rhythm and drag from the legs.

Visibility:

- Usually unavailable from above-water side clips.
- Wide knee bend may sometimes be visible, but kick depth/timing and 2-beat
  rhythm are not reliable without better footage.

Allowed above-water behavior:

- Avoid "2-beat vs 6-beat" verdicts unless the clip truly supports it.
- For visible wide legs, frame as drag, not lack of power.

Preferred prescription:

- Quiet legs focal point.
- Skate with a gentle toe flick.
- 2-beat kick concepts for distance only when timing is visible or taught in
  person.

## Prompt Decision Tree

1. Is the clip usable for this aspect?
2. If no, return `unclear` or unavailable.
3. If yes, choose the closed verdict.
4. Attach confidence based on visibility, not importance.
5. Let code-side grading rank the verdict by discipline.
6. In the final summary, prescribe one TI focal point or drill.

## Drill Prescription Map

| Fault | First prescription |
| --- | --- |
| lifted head, sinking hips, sinking legs | Superman Glide with eyes down and soft neck |
| unstable side position, limited rotation | Zen Skate / side balance |
| short entry or rushed lead hand | Patient Lead Hand / SpearSkate |
| timing or rotation issue around the switch | SpearSwitch / Switch drills |
| tense or dropped recovery | Shark Fin or Zipper / fingertip-drag |
| wide kick / visible leg drag | Quiet legs in skate, small toe flick |
| catch/pull issue from above-water clip | unavailable; request underwater footage or in-person coach |

## Output Standards

- No stroke rate, SPM, cadence, degree values, or precise timing unless a
  validated deterministic component produces a hedged value.
- No fake praise. Strengths require evidence.
- No "pull harder" or "kick harder" as the primary fix.
- No more than three priority fixes; one high-confidence foundation fix is often
  better.
- Always make clear this is automated analysis and not a substitute for a human
  coach.

## Implementation Notes

- Shared prompt block should state the TI order: balance -> streamline ->
  propulsion.
- Each aspect prompt should include only the TI cues relevant to that aspect.
- Aggregator prompt should own the drill prescription map because it is the layer
  that turns findings into user-facing next steps.
- `grade()` remains the deterministic discipline re-ranker. TI changes should not
  make the VLM "see" different facts for sprint vs distance; they should only
  change wording, drill choice, and priority.
